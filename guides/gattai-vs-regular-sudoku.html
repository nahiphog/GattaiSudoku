Gattai Sudoku vs Regular Sudoku

A side-by-side comparison of the classic 9×9 format and its overlapping two-grid cousin.

If you already know how to solve standard Sudoku, you're most of the way to solving Gattai Sudoku. But the overlapping layout creates meaningful differences in strategy, clue design, and difficulty. Here's what changes.

More cells, more information

A standard Sudoku has 81 cells. Gattai has 126 — about 56% more. With more cells come more constraints: every digit you place in the shared 6×6 region must satisfy two grids' worth of row, column, and box restrictions. This means:

  • More given clues are needed to force a unique solution. Standard Sudoku can be uniquely solvable with 17 clues; a Gattai puzzle typically needs 28 or more.
  • Deductions chain further. A candidate eliminated by a Grid 1 interaction might also open up a placement in Grid 2, and vice versa.
  • Scanning takes more practice. You need to track two sets of houses simultaneously for the overlapping cells.

The shared region changes everything

In standard Sudoku, every cell belongs to exactly one row, one column, and one 3×3 box. In Gattai, the 36 cells of the shared 6×6 belong to two rows (one per grid), two columns, and one of four shared 3×3 boxes. A digit in a shared cell must not conflict with:

  • The row, column, and box constraints of Grid 1
  • And the row, column, and box constraints of Grid 2
Practical effect: The overlap makes "hidden" techniques more powerful. A Hidden Single in the shared 6×6 might be hidden in Grid 1's row but naked in Grid 2's column — or the opposite. Good Gattai solvers learn to scan both grids' constraints at once.

Clue symmetry

The puzzles on this site use rotational-pair clue design. When a clue is removed, its 180° partner within the same grid is also removed. This creates a visually balanced grid and ensures that every puzzle has a well-defined difficulty rating. Standard Sudoku has no such constraint — clue patterns can be arbitrary.

Difficulty comparison

Gattai puzzles at the same technique tier tend to feel slightly easier than their standard counterparts, because the extra constraints in the overlap give you more information to work with. A standard Sudoku with 28 givens might be Medium; a Gattai with 28 givens is likely Beginner or Easy. That said, the hardest Gattai puzzles (Friday–Sunday) still demand skilled logical chaining.

Which one should you play?

  • If you're new to Sudoku: Start with standard Sudoku, then try Gattai Beginner/Easy puzzles once you're comfortable.
  • If you're a regular Sudoku solver looking for a fresh challenge: Gattai is a natural next step — familiar rules, new spatial thinking.
  • If you enjoy variant Sudoku (Killer, Samurai, Arrow): Gattai's overlapping-grid structure is a unique twist you'll likely enjoy.
